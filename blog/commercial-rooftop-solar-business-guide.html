Commercial rooftop solar: a decision guide

Evaluate the project through load profile, roof and electrical rights, operations, safety, approvals, financial assumptions, and long-term ownership.

By Your Energy Editorial Team8 min read

Commercial solar can align well with offices, factories, warehouses, schools, hospitals, shops, and institutions because many consume energy during daylight. Yet a project should not be approved from the total monthly bill or roof area alone. Demand profile, tariff structure, sanctioned load, operational risk, ownership, and technical integration all matter.

Quick answer

Request interval or time-of-day load data where available, at least twelve months of bills, roof and structural records, electrical single-line information, operating schedule, planned expansion, and current approval requirements. Model self-consumption and export separately.

Understand the load profile

Monthly units show volume but not timing. Two businesses can consume the same annual energy while offering very different solar value. A daytime factory may use nearly all generation on site, while a warehouse with a small daytime load may export more. Record weekday, weekend, seasonal, shift, shutdown, and holiday patterns.

Read the complete electricity bill

  • Energy consumption and tariff category.
  • Sanctioned or contract demand and maximum demand.
  • Demand charges, power factor, time-of-day components, and penalties.
  • Open-access or multiple-meter arrangements where relevant.
  • Recent tariff or operational changes.

Solar generally affects energy purchases differently from demand or fixed charges. A proposal should not apply one simple percentage to the total bill without explaining the billing model.

Confirm roof and site rights

Check who owns the building, who controls the roof, lease duration, landlord consent, lender rights, fire access, insurance, future expansion, and responsibility for roof repairs. For sheet roofs, assess condition, remaining life, corrosion, load, fasteners, and whether replacement should happen before solar.

Model economics transparently

A decision model should state annual generation assumptions, degradation treatment, self-consumption, export credit, tariff escalation, downtime, maintenance, inverter or battery replacement where applicable, financing cost, taxes, insurance, and project life. Show a range or sensitivity rather than one guaranteed payback number.

Capex, financing, and third-party models

In a capital-purchase model, the business owns the asset and carries performance and maintenance responsibilities according to the contract. Financing changes cash flow and effective cost. Third-party energy-sale or lease structures allocate ownership and risk differently. Review tenure, tariff escalation, minimum commitments, roof access, early termination, insurance, taxes, lender rights, and end-of-term treatment with professional advisers.

Approval and metering

Applicable rules depend on state, DISCOM, consumer category, capacity, sanctioned load, and proposed arrangement. Confirm technical feasibility, metering, protection, inspection, and commissioning requirements from current official sources. Residential subsidy assumptions should not be applied to a commercial connection.

Contract and handover essentials

  • Frozen equipment schedule and controlled substitution process.
  • Design deliverables, approvals, responsibilities, exclusions, and change orders.
  • Project schedule linked to customer and utility dependencies.
  • Quality plan, safety plan, testing, commissioning, and acceptance criteria.
  • Generation model with assumptions, not a vague guarantee.
  • Warranty process, response route, spare strategy, and AMC scope.
  • Drawings, serial numbers, monitoring ownership, manuals, and staff training.

Questions management should ask

  1. What proportion of generation is expected to be self-consumed?
  2. What happens during weekly holidays and seasonal shutdowns?
  3. Which bill components are assumed to reduce?
  4. What roof or electrical upgrade is excluded?
  5. What production shutdown is required for interconnection?
  6. Who carries approval, delay, safety, and performance risk?
  7. How will performance be reviewed after commissioning?
